You Were Never Supposed to Do This Alone: Motherhood, Mental Health, and the Pressure to Manage

Online therapy for women in New Jersey and New York

Book a Free Consultation

There's a particular kind of loneliness that can come with struggling in motherhood: looking around and believing everyone else knows how to do this.

Other mothers seem to manage. They get out of the house, return to work, remember the appointments, and somehow appear comfortable in a role that has left you feeling overwhelmed or unfamiliar to yourself.

You may wonder: Why does this feel so hard for me?

Admitting that you're struggling can feel especially difficult when motherhood is presented as something women are naturally equipped to do. We hear about "a mother's instinct" as though knowing how to care for a child—and how to cope with everything that comes with it—should simply arrive with the baby.

If it's supposed to be innate, shouldn't we glide into it?

That expectation leaves very little room for the reality of becoming a mother.

Love doesn't make the transition effortless

You can love your child deeply and find motherhood difficult. You can have wanted this pregnancy and feel overwhelmed by what follows. You can feel grateful, angry, joyful, lonely, and uncertain, sometimes within the same afternoon.

Motherhood can change your body, sleep, relationships, finances, work, and sense of identity. You're learning about your child while also getting to know yourself in a different role.

An instinct to care doesn't give you unlimited energy, a support system, or immediate answers. Some parts of parenting become more familiar through experience. Others require information, practice, and help.

Struggling with that process doesn't tell us how much you love your child.

When perfectionism comes into parenthood

If you've spent much of your life being capable, prepared, and dependable, motherhood can bring you into uncomfortable territory.

You may be used to working harder when something feels uncertain. Research more. Plan better. Anticipate the problem before it happens.

But parenting doesn't offer a reliable equation in which enough effort guarantees the outcome you want.

A difficult night can start to feel like evidence that you did something wrong. Conflicting advice can turn an ordinary decision into a test of whether you're a good mother. Needing a break can bring guilt before you've even had the chance to rest.

Research has linked some forms of perfectionism, particularly concern about mistakes and harsh self-evaluation, with depression and anxiety during pregnancy and postpartum. That association doesn't mean perfectionism determines your experience, but it helps explain why an already demanding transition may feel especially unforgiving.

The question becomes less about what you or your baby need and more about whether you're doing it correctly.

When independence is part of how you measure your worth, asking for help may feel like admitting defeat. You might think you should be able to handle it because other mothers seem to—or because you chose to become a parent.

Choosing motherhood does not mean agreeing to have no needs.

We see other mothers' functioning, not their full experience

From the outside, it's easy to confuse getting things done with feeling well.

You see someone at work, at school pickup, or posting a smiling photo. You may not see the anxiety, the relationship strain, the paid help, or the family member who makes that day possible.

You also may not see how much effort it takes for her to appear okay.

When mothers feel pressure to hide their struggles, that silence can reinforce the belief that everyone else is managing comfortably. Each woman sees other people's outward competence and compares it with her own private distress.

Being more honest about motherhood creates room for a fuller picture—one in which love and difficulty can exist together.

The missing support is part of the story

Caregiving takes time, energy, and resources. Yet many families are expected to manage it within a small household, alongside paid work and the ongoing demands of daily life.

Family may live far away. Friends may be stretched thin. Childcare may be unaffordable. A partner may be loving and involved while also exhausted or unavailable because of work. Some mothers are parenting without a partner at all.

Across cultures and generations, childrearing has often involved extended family and wider networks of care. That support has never been universal or equally available, and the past was not an easier or safer time for every mother. Still, it matters that caring for children can be shared work.

When care happens behind a closed front door, the amount of labor inside can become invisible. A mother may be struggling under an unsustainable load while believing the problem is her inability to organize herself.

We also need room to talk about the economic conditions surrounding motherhood: pressure to remain productive, inadequate leave, expensive childcare, and caregiving work that is essential but often unpaid and undervalued.

These are ways our economic system can place demands on families without providing the support those demands require. Calling the result a personal failure misses a large part of the picture.

Sometimes the most useful question is: What am I being asked to carry, and what support is missing?

Understanding the context can soften self-blame

Recognizing these pressures doesn't immediately create childcare, more paid leave, or nearby family. It can, however, change how you understand your struggle.

You may be exhausted because your responsibilities exceed your available energy. You may feel isolated because you need more connection than your current circumstances provide. You may need practical help alongside emotional support.

That understanding can create room for both compassion and action.

Support might mean having a more specific conversation about who owns household responsibilities, asking someone to bring dinner rather than visit, finding a parent group where you can speak honestly, or seeking professional care.

Not every option will be accessible. Having limited support is not another failure to correct. We can acknowledge those limits while looking for what might make life more manageable.

When it's time to reach out

Maternal mental health includes more than the moments when everything becomes unbearable.

Persistent anxiety, sadness, irritability, guilt, or difficulty functioning deserve attention during pregnancy and after birth. Depression and anxiety are treatable, and you don't need to wait until you reach a breaking point to speak with a therapist or healthcare provider.

Social pressures and lack of support matter. Biology, mental health history, physical health, and other individual circumstances matter too. Understanding the broader context and receiving treatment can happen together.

Therapy can offer space to talk about the parts of motherhood you've felt afraid to admit: resentment, uncertainty, changes in your identity, or the feeling that you've disappeared beneath everyone else's needs.

It can also help you examine perfectionistic expectations, practice asking for support, and work through the guilt that sometimes follows taking care of yourself.

You don't have to perform being okay in order to deserve care.

Making room for the mother, too

As a therapist and a mother, I care about creating space for the full experience of this transition. Your experience may look very different from mine or anyone else's. There is room to understand it without measuring you against an idealized version of motherhood.

You can be a loving mother who needs rest. A capable mother who needs guidance. A grateful mother who is having a hard time.

Your needs belong in the picture.
