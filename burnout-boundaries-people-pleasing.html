Therapy for Burnout, Boundaries, and People-Pleasing

Online therapy for women in New Jersey and New York

Book a Free Consultation

You're allowed to need something, too.

You're someone people count on. You notice what needs to happen, remember the details, and make room for other people's feelings. You may genuinely enjoy caring for others.

But lately, you're exhausted. Small requests feel like too much. You feel guilty resting and resentful that so much falls to you. You wonder why the care you give so readily doesn't always seem to come back.

You might keep telling yourself you need to manage your time better, be more grateful, or get back on top of things. Meanwhile, your own needs keep moving to the bottom of the list.

"Take care of yourself" can feel like one more demand.

Women receive a lot of conflicting messages: work hard, be present for your family, nurture your relationships, keep everything organized—and somehow find time to rest, exercise, and take care of yourself.

We're told we can't pour from an empty cup, but rarely given enough support to put the cup down.

For women navigating pregnancy, birth, and parenthood, that contradiction can feel especially sharp. Your body, sleep, relationships, and daily life may have changed profoundly, while expectations remain much the same.

You can love your child and find parenting arduous. You can feel grateful and need relief. There needs to be room for the whole experience.

Whether or not you're a parent, therapy can be a place to examine what you're carrying, what is realistically sustainable, and where something needs to change.

Caring for others shouldn't require abandoning yourself.

People-pleasing can be difficult to recognize when it looks like being thoughtful, dependable, or easygoing. Sometimes you say yes because you want to. Other times, saying no brings up guilt, fear of conflict, or worry that someone will think you're selfish.

Over time, repeatedly setting aside your needs can leave you depleted and disconnected from what you actually want.

We'll explore the difference between care you freely choose and care you feel obligated to provide. We'll also question the messages that tie being a "good" woman, partner, friend, or parent to being endlessly available.

Your needs deserve consideration alongside everyone else's.

Boundaries involve more than knowing what to say.

You may already know you need boundaries. The harder part is tolerating what comes afterward: someone's disappointment, an awkward conversation, or the urge to take it all back.

In therapy, we can work on:

  • Recognizing your needs before you reach exhaustion or resentment.
  • Understanding the beliefs that make rest, asking for help, or saying no feel difficult.
  • Communicating limits clearly, with less overexplaining.
  • Making specific requests for support and examining how responsibilities are shared.
  • Tolerating guilt and disappointment without automatically reversing your decision.
  • Making more room for rest, connection, enjoyment, and your own priorities.

You can remain loving and supportive while having limits. Other people may need time to adjust to those limits, and we can make space for how uncomfortable that feels.

A fuller life has room for difficult feelings.

Some parts of life are painful, and some demands genuinely need to change. We'll work on recognizing the difference between discomfort you can learn to move through and circumstances that are asking too much of you.

My goal is to help you build the insight, skills, and capacity to stay connected to yourself as you make choices about your life—even when those choices bring uncertainty, sadness, or guilt.

There can be room for joy and grief, love and frustration, caring for others and caring for yourself.
